Fiery Void is an online adaptation of the Babylon 5 Wars tabletop game by Agents of Gaming (heads bowed to you, AoG!).

So there is no rulebook for Fiery Void itself: the rules are freely available under the name Advent of Galactic Wars, hosted at the B5Wars Vault. If you know those rules, you should be able to play Fiery Void once you have got to grips with the online interface.

The game will seem very complex at first, but don't worry: start with the basics and dig deeper at your own pace, and you will be a lion of the galaxy soon enough. The Starter Guide walks you through a first game.

For your first game, we suggest asking a more experienced player for a tutorial - in the in-game chat, or on our Discord (the link is on the main page). It shouldn't be hard to find a volunteer to explain the basics, and any other questions about the game are welcome on Discord too!

  • A lot has been automated compared to B5 Wars, such as dice rolling. Players have a little less control over certain minutiae, but overall the game is more streamlined.
  • By default the game is played on a fixed, rectangular map. Nothing about the map's edges is enforced - it is up to the players to make sure ships leaving the map behave as disengaged.
  • Fiery Void does not enforce the standard fleet design rules, but you can still check a fleet against them with the Check button in Fleet Selection (see Fleet Checker).
  • The game mechanics use d100 rolls instead of d20, so most values are shown as percentages (1 point on a d20 is 5%).
  • There are no separate Power and Electronic Warfare phases. Both are part of a single Initial Orders phase, along with ballistic firing, which happens after the initiative roll.

Electronic Warfare

  • CCEW provides a lock on all enemy fighters within 10 hexes.
  • Most activities that need several EW points can be done in fractions. Blanket DEW, for example, gives 5% per 4 points allocated, so 3 points give around 4%.
  • Disruption EW: enemy target locks are affected equally by the fraction of DIST used - 1 point of DIST (which costs 3 EW points) against 3 separate OEW locks reduces each by 0.33 points.
  • Disruption EW: CCEW counts as one OEW target and is affected accordingly.
  • Disruption EW: a target lock weaker than 1 point but worth at least half a point gives a half-lock - range penalties are multiplied by 1.5 instead of being doubled.
  • DEW below 0: the B5 Wars rule that only a ship's own DEW can take its profile below 0 does not apply in Fiery Void.
  • EW is covered in more detail under ELINT & Electronic Warfare.

Movement

  • Snap turns are made as consecutive but separate turns. The turn delay is taken from the first turn only, so shorten the first turn to shorten the snap turn.
  • Extended turns - turns spread across several game turns - are not implemented.

Firing and weapons

Firing order
Each weapon has a priority number that decides when it fires, except ramming attacks, which always go first. Players cannot change the order.
Target section
Chosen automatically from the target's profiles and remaining structure. All fire from the same ship hits the same section, and primary sections are avoided while there is an alternative.
System hit
In-arc systems are hit before off-arc ones. Damage is assumed to come from the direction of the shot.
Targeting fighters
The algorithm minimises the expected overall damage. You can target an individual fighter by hand, but it counts as a called shot, at -40% to hit.
Fighter dropouts
Happen during critical hit resolution, after firing at ships.
Interception
Automatic, taking on the most powerful incoming shots first. Weapons with a 1-turn recharge intercept automatically if they have not fired; others must be selected by hand (see Interception).
Ballistic damage
Resolved during normal fire resolution at the end of the turn, rather than in a separate ballistic damage sub-phase.
Fighter escorts
Fighters can intercept ballistics aimed at a ship they are escorting if they start and end their movement in its hex.
Ballistics & jammers
Power and missile launches are simultaneous, so disabling a jammer affects missile launches from the next turn, not the current one.
Multi-mode weapons
Simplified: they may switch modes freely (Guardian Array) or be boosted for free in Initial Orders (EA Interceptors).
Firing modes
Always visible, so opponents can see missile types immediately.

Ship windows

The ship window is the Ship Control Sheet (SCS) that opens when you right-click a unit.

  • Drag the title bar to move the window. On a computer, the next window you open on that side of the screen appears where you left the last one (until you reload the page).
  • Drag the corner grip to resize the window. The grip is on the corner facing away from the edge the window is docked to (bottom-right for a left-hand window, bottom-left for a right-hand one), so you always drag outwards to make it bigger; a right-hand window stops growing at the left edge of the screen. The size you choose is used by every later window on that side of the screen - the two sides are remembered separately - and is kept on your device for next time, so you can set it once to suit your screen. On a touchscreen, drag it with your finger.
  • Double-click the corner grip or the title bar to reset the window to its normal size (double-tap on a touchscreen). The title bar does it too, so you can always get back even if a window is so large its corner is off the screen.
  • Phones and tablets: windows are scaled to fit your screen automatically before any resizing of your own, with extra size in portrait, where space is tightest. Anything that does not fit scrolls inside the window, and the title bar stays pinned to the top so it can always be dragged.
  • Hit Chart / Ship Art / Ship Stats / Notes: click a button to open its panel, and click anywhere outside to close it (Ship Stats and Notes also peek open when you point at them). Ship Stats shows this turn's actual turn cost, turn delay, profile and initiative, with any figure the current situation has changed shown in yellow.
  • Ship Art shows the unit's artwork in full colour in place of its sections; click the button again to go back.
  • Point at a system to show its details in the info panel. On a touchscreen, press and hold - a plain tap is the action (power, firing, repair and so on).
  • Click the artwork behind the sections to show the unit's own details rather than a system's.

During deployment

  • Shift + left-click deploys a ship straight into a hex other units already occupy (long press on a touchscreen). It must still be a valid deployment: fighters and mines can stack with ships, but ships cannot be deployed with other ships.
  • Double left-click selects a single unit in a hex straight away when you already have a deployable unit selected (if the hex holds several units you still pick from the list). It makes selecting quicker when your selected unit is a fighter flight or a mine.
  • Right-click the + or - movement icons to set the ship's speed to the maximum (10) or minimum (0) in one click.

The Info Panel is the tabbed panel along the bottom-left of the battle screen. Its six tabs share one body, so only one is on screen at a time: COMBAT LOG, FLEET INFO, GAME CHAT, CHAT, DECLARATIONS and SAVE FLEET.

Click a tab to switch to it. A tab is only a view - switching tabs never changes your orders.

Sizing the panel

  • Drag the panel's top edge up or down to set its height. The edge lights up when you point at it (on a touchscreen, drag it with your finger).
  • The chevron (▲ / ▼) at the right-hand end of the tab strip, or a double-click on the top edge, switches between the compact and the tall panel. Opening the tall panel also tucks the initiative drawer out of the way.
  • The compact and tall heights are remembered separately and kept on your device for next time - so you can have one size for glancing at the log and another for going through a whole turn.
  • Expanding or collapsing the panel leaves you on the tab you were reading.
  • On phones, and on short landscape screens, the panel shrinks to its tab strip until you tap the chevron, and only the selected tab is drawn while it is closed - there is not room for six tabs and a map.
  • Right-clicking inside the panel no longer opens your browser's own menu, so a right-click aimed at a unit that lands slightly low does not cover the game with it. The game's own right-click actions still work (a right-click on a fleet-list row still opens that unit's ship window). The browser menu is still there in a text box, so you can paste into chat, and when you have selected some text, so you can copy a log line out to Discord.

COMBAT LOG

What happened, this turn and in every turn before it.

  • During a replay the log fills in live as the turn plays out. The rest of the time it shows the printed log of whichever turn you have selected.
  • ◀ TURN n ▶ steps back and forth through the turns; the arrows grey out at either end. The Live button appears only while you are looking at an older turn, and takes you back to the current one.
  • Sort: Resolution is the order the game actually resolved the fire in - what you want while following a replay. Attacker and Target regroup the same entries by who was shooting or who was being shot at, which is usually easier to read afterwards. On a narrow screen the three buttons become a dropdown.
  • All / Mine / Enemy chooses whose fire to show.
  • Hits shows only fire that scored at least one hit, for when a big turn is mostly misses.
  • Find filters the turn down to a ship or a weapon by name.
  • The readout to the left of the Find box names the current phase and, while a filter is on, how many fire groups it is hiding - so a filtered log never looks like a quiet turn.
  • Reading the colours: ship names are pale, and the target of each shot is drawn in its team colour, since who was shot at is what the eye looks for. Damage is red, criticals are amber, and shield absorption is blue.

FLEET INFO

Every fleet in the battle, one block per player.

  • Each block's header shows the team (coloured by allegiance), the player's name, the fleet's current / base points, and a chip saying where that player is up to: Orders committed, Waiting for Movement orders, Surrendered T5, or Deploys T3 for a slot that has not arrived yet. Click the header to collapse or expand that fleet.
  • Click a column head (Ship Name, Class, Type, Ini, Value) to sort by it; click again to reverse, and a third time to go back to initiative order. Your choice is remembered.
  • Left-click a unit row to scroll the map to that unit.
  • Right-click a unit row to open its ship window without selecting the unit or moving the map (long press on a touchscreen). You can also click the ⓘ that appears at the right-hand end of a row when you point at it - the easier target on a phone.
  • Units that are off the board but still yours - docked flights and reinforcements waiting in hyperspace - open their ship window on either click, since there is nowhere to scroll to. For a docked flight, that window is the only way to see what is actually in the bay.
  • Destroyed and jumped units are inert: no highlight and no window. They are gone from the battle, and there is nothing left to inspect.
  • On map only hides everything not currently on the board - destroyed, jumped, docked, and reinforcements still in hyperspace - and hides a whole fleet block when nothing in it is left to show. Mines are on the map, so they stay.
  • The team dropdown narrows the list to one team.
  • The readout recounts fleets and units against whatever filters are on, so it always describes what is actually on screen.
  • The FAQ, Ammo & Options and Factions buttons open those documents in this Data Archive window, over the game.

GAME CHAT and CHAT

  • GAME CHAT is this battle only, and everyone in the game can read it - the place to agree a house rule, warn an opponent you will be slow, or ask what a system does.
  • CHAT is the site-wide channel, the same one as on the main page.
  • A tab turns amber when an unread message arrives on it, so you can leave both closed and still not miss anything.

DECLARATIONS

A read-out of the orders currently on the table. This is the tab to check before you commit: an unspent EW point or a weapon aimed at the wrong ship is far easier to spot here than in a dozen separate ship windows.

  • Side: Own / Enemy - whose orders to list.
  • Show: EW / Fire - electronic warfare allocations, or firing orders.
  • By: Source / Target - grouped under the unit doing it, or the unit it is being done to. So Own + Fire + By Source is your ships and what each of them is shooting at, while Enemy + Fire + By Target is your ships and what is shooting at them. The same pairing works for EW: what you are emitting, versus what is being pointed at you.
  • The EW view lists every point each unit is emitting or receiving, with a per-unit total, so you can see at a glance whether a ship still has EW left to allocate. The Fire view reads like "4x Heavy Laser → Vorchan 45-60%": how many guns, at whom, and the to-hit range.
  • Briefing (far right) replaces the panel with the game's name, the rules of engagement in play (Friendly fire, Mines, Reinforcements, Desperate) and the scenario text. Press it again to go back to the view you were on. The three filter groups dim while it is up, because none of them means anything for a briefing.
  • This tab only ever shows what your own game data already contains, so it cannot reveal an opponent's orders any earlier than the rest of the interface does.

SAVE FLEET

Saves your surviving ships - with their enhancements, remaining ammunition, battle damage and critical effects - as a reusable fleet list. Load it in Fleet Selection to carry a campaign on into the next battle. See Battle Damage & Saving Fleets for exactly what is and is not carried over.

The Ruler Tool measures the distance between any two hexes on the map, and shows whether line of sight between them is blocked.

  1. Turn it on with the R key, or the eye button on the right-hand side of the screen.
  2. Left-click the hex to measure from, then move the mouse around the map to check the distance and line of sight to other hexes.
  3. Right-click to reset the starting hex.

Fiery Void games often run over days or weeks, so it is easy to miss the moment one starts waiting on you. If you like, the Fiery Void bot can send you a direct message on Discord whenever one of your games needs you.

It is completely opt-in: nothing is ever sent unless you link your Discord account, and you can unlink at any time.

Before you start

Two things must be true, or the bot cannot reach you:

  • You must be a member of the Fiery Void Discord server - Discord only lets a bot message people who share a server with it.
  • You must allow DMs from that server: right-click the Fiery Void server icon → Privacy Settings → turn on Allow direct messages from server members.

Don't worry that the bot always shows as offline in the member list - that is normal. It only sends messages; it never logs in or reads your chat.

Setting it up

A one-off that takes a minute:

  1. Find your Discord user ID. In Discord go to User Settings → Developer and turn on Developer Mode. Then right-click your own name (in a chat or the member list) and choose Copy User ID. It is a long number of 17-20 digits - not your username.
  2. Paste it into Fiery Void. On the main page, click Set-Up Discord Notifications in the top-right corner, paste your Discord user ID into the box, and click Send verification code.
  3. Check your Discord DMs. The Fiery Void bot sends you a 6-digit code, valid for 10 minutes.
  4. Enter the code back on the notifications page and click Verify. You're linked.
  5. Optionally, click Send test ping to prove a DM reaches you.
Why is there a code?

Your Discord user ID is not a secret - anyone who shares a server with you can copy it. Without a check, somebody could type your ID into their Fiery Void account and have the bot send you reminders for their games. Because the code is only ever sent to the true owner of the ID, only you can finish the link. A Discord account can be linked to one Fiery Void account at a time.

How it works in practice

  • You are messaged when a game needs something from you - exactly the same condition that highlights a game in your games list. That covers every phase, including Fleet Selection, Deployment, Initial Orders, Movement and Firing.
  • The DM names the game, the turn and the phase, and links straight to the game.
  • You get one DM each time a game becomes blocked on you - not one per phase. Once a game is waiting on you nothing more can happen until you act, so you will not be spammed.
  • You are never pinged for your own moves, and not while you are actively playing: if the game has seen you in the last 5 minutes, it assumes you are already there. Two players trading moves in real time get no DMs at all.
  • Nothing is sent for games that have finished or been surrendered.

Turning it off

Go back to Set-Up Discord Notifications and click Unlink - the pings stop immediately. To use a different Discord account, unlink first and then verify the new ID.

Troubleshooting

No code arrived
Nearly always your DM privacy setting, or you are not in the Fiery Void Discord server. Fix those (see Before you start) and request another code.
"Incorrect code"
Make sure you are using the newest code. After a few wrong attempts the code is cancelled for safety - just request a fresh one.
"That code has expired"
Codes last 10 minutes; request another.
"Please wait a few seconds"
There is a short cooldown between code requests, so the feature cannot be abused to spam someone.
DMs closed
A test ping may show up as a mention in the #turn-pings channel instead, where that fallback has been set up. Opening your DMs is the better fix.

The Online Ladder is there mainly to help players set up well-balanced, interesting games - and to provide some bragging rights along the way.

It works like a handicap in golf: the difference in rating between the two players is added as a percentage bonus to the lower-rated player's points. A difference of 5 rating means the lower-rated player gets 5% extra points!

Setting up a ladder game

  • Create a game as usual and tick Ladder Game. The View Ladder button beside it shows the current ratings, and can work out the points difference against a particular player and fill the team slots with those values.
  • Or set up an open ladder game without it, setting the points in the team slots as usual: when a player takes the other slot, their points are adjusted automatically.
  • Either way, decide the details of the game (points, map, standard or simultaneous movement and so on), then create it. Ladder games are competitive matches, so only two players can take part, with one slot per team.

Results

When the game ends with one player surrendering (after at least one whole turn has been played), the winner's ranking goes up by one on the ladder and the loser's goes down by the same amount.

To see a player's match history over the last three months - your own included - click View Ladder on the main page, then click their name.

A saved fleet is a reusable shopping list: the units you bought, the enhancements and ammunition you gave them, and - if you want - the battle damage and critical effects they are carrying.

That last part means you can save a fleet at the end of one battle and start the next with exactly the ships that survived it, dents and all - which is what makes linked scenarios and campaigns possible. You can also apply damage by hand in Fleet Selection, to set up a scenario without having fought the previous game.

Saving a fleet in Fleet Selection

Build your fleet as normal, click Save Fleet in the Your Fleet panel, give the fleet a name and click Save. It is then available in this and every future game (as long as you have enough points to buy it) from the Load a Fleet menu.

  • Terrain is never saved, even if you placed it yourself - a fleet list is ships, and the scenario provides the scenery.
  • Mines bought in bulk are saved as a bulk purchase, so ten mines come back as ten mines rather than one.

Loading a saved fleet

Pick a slot, open Load a Fleet and click the fleet you want: the whole fleet is bought into your slot in one go. If the fleet costs more points than you have left, the load is refused outright rather than partly applied. Units the scenario does not allow - mines in a game without mines, or units that entered service after the game's In-Service Date - are left out, the rest of the fleet loads, and you are told what was left out.

Sharing fleets and loading by ID

Every saved fleet has a unique ID number. If the fleet is marked as shared - you can set this when you save it, or toggle it later with the padlock symbol in the list - you can give its ID to another player. They load it by typing the number into the Load Fleet by #ID box and pressing Enter (or Go, on a phone). Fleets that have not been shared can only be loaded by the player who saved them.

Saving a fleet from a game in progress

In any game, open the SAVE FLEET tab of the Info Panel (beside COMBAT LOG, DECLARATIONS and the chats). It tells you how many of your units will be saved, and Save Current Fleet saves them as a normal saved fleet - but with all their accumulated damage and critical effects attached.

  • The tab is available in every phase, and in finished games and replays too, so you can go back to a completed battle and save the winning side's survivors.
  • Only survivors are saved. Destroyed ships, ships that docked into a hangar and left the battle, and mines you have already laid are all left out; the summary line says how many were excluded.
  • Fighters are never saved as wrecks. A flight of six that lost two craft is saved as a flight of four damaged fighters - and since you are buying fewer craft next time, it costs proportionally less.
  • By default only lasting wounds are saved. Tick "Also save temporary critical effects" to carry one-turn effects over as well: they apply during turn 1 of the next battle and then expire. Marine and boarding markers are never saved - they belong to the battle you just fought.
  • The fleet is named after the game and turn by default (for example Second Contact T7), which is what tells two saves of the same fleet apart in the list. Type over it if you prefer.

Loading a damaged fleet

A fleet carrying battle damage or critical effects has a spanner icon in the fleet list. When you load it you are asked two separate questions: Include saved battle damage and Include saved critical effects. Both are ticked by default, and each is only offered if the fleet actually has that kind of state. So the same fleet can be reloaded pristine, damaged but uncritted, critted but repaired, or exactly as it ended the last battle - your choice, every time you load it.

Applying battle damage by hand

In Fleet Selection, any unit you have bought can be damaged before the battle starts. No rule gates this - it is on the honour system, and meant for scenarios, campaigns and "what if" games. Open the ship's window and:

  • Damage a system: click its icon to open a small menu with Damage and Critical Effects sections (on a system that can take refits, a gold Enhancements section sits above them - see System Enhancements). The number shown is the remaining structure boxes, not the damage - the same number you would read off the SCS. Use the - and + buttons, type a value, or roll the mouse wheel over the box (up repairs, down damages). Tick Destroy to knock the system out completely; untick it and the system comes back at the value you had dialled in before.
  • Damage a section's Structure: click the coloured section header bar (the one showing the section's name and structure total) - Structure has no icon of its own, so that bar is its icon. Destroying a section's Structure destroys the systems in that section, exactly as it would in a real game. (Vree saucers are the exception: their outer structure blocks are a ring around the disc, not the compartments the systems sit in, so losing one does not take its systems with it.)
  • Add critical effects: under the damage row is a Critical Effects list. Use + Add effect… to choose an effect that system can genuinely suffer, and the - / + buttons to change how many of it the system carries. Tick All to widen the list with effects that apply to any system. Some effects only ever apply once, and the + button greys out when you reach that limit. Dropping an effect to zero leaves its row on screen, so you can put it straight back.
  • Damage a fighter flight: click the flight's health bar. You get one row per craft, so you can wound individual fighters, plus Apply Fighter 1 to all to copy the first row across the flight. Critical effects are set once for the whole flight, not per craft. There is no Destroy option - a dead fighter is simply one fewer in the flight, so use Edit on the flight to reduce its size instead.
  • Damage bulk-bought mines: click the mine's structure bar. You get one row per mine in the purchase, plus Apply Mine 1 to all. Mines take structure damage only and cannot be destroyed - a mine you lost is one you did not buy.

Things worth knowing

  • Any unit carrying pre-battle damage or critical effects has a spanner icon in your fleet list, so you can see at a glance which ships are going in wounded.
  • Damage does not make a ship cheaper. A crippled Omega costs exactly what a fresh one does. (A shrunken fighter flight does cost less, but only because it is fewer fighters.)
  • Clicking Ready with damaged units in your fleet adds a note to the confirmation, so nobody readies a wounded fleet by accident.
  • If you Edit a flight and change its size - or change how many mines are in a bulk purchase - any damage you applied to it is cleared, and you are told so. The damage is stored per craft, and there is no honest way to re-map it onto a different number of craft.
  • Everything you apply here goes into the game exactly as if it had been inflicted on an earlier turn: your opponents can see it once the game starts, and the ships behave accordingly from Deployment onwards.

Many factions have Breaching Pods and Grappling Claws, which carry marines that can board enemy ships.

Buying breaching pods

In Fleet Selection you can buy one pod for every Medium Ship or HCV with hangar space for medium fighters, heavy fighters or assault shuttles; two for every such Capital Ship; and four for Enormous units or bases. Assault ships (those with "Assault" in their name) can carry double these allowances, if they have the hangar space.

Attaching

  • In battle, a pod can try to attach to an enemy ship in the same hex and deliver its marines on a mission - Capture Ship, Sabotage or Rescue - during the Firing Phase.
  • A pod rolls to attach on a d10, like a normal weapon rolling to hit, but the calculation is very different. Attaching succeeds automatically if the pod is moving faster than its target and the speed difference is no more than the pod's thrust rating; if the difference is greater than its thrust rating, it simply cannot attach. If the target is moving faster, each point of speed difference is -10% to attach.
  • Pods cannot attach to ships with Advanced Armor, and certain factions, such as the Llort, have +1 to attach.

How many can attach

  • Attachment is limited per structure section: at most two Breaching Pods may attach to any one section. So a hull's total follows how many sections it actually has - a Capital Ship takes 2 per facing, an HCV 2 forward and 2 aft, a Medium Ship 2 in total, and a Drazi Stormfalcon (which has no aft structure) takes 6 rather than 8.
  • On a vessel with exterior structure blocks, the Primary section can only be reached once the exterior structure facing the pod has been destroyed, and it then holds two pods for every destroyed exterior section - so a Capital Ship that has lost both its sides can carry four on its Primary, two through each breach. Medium Ships, LCVs and OSATs have no exterior structure blocks at all, so for them Primary is simply the hull and holds the usual two.
  • An overall ceiling by hull size applies on top of this, so a vessel with an unusual number of sections (such as a six-sided Vree saucer) never exceeds what its class allows: 12 for bases and Enormous units, 8 for Capital Ships, 4 for HCVs and 2 for Medium Ships and smaller. LCVs and OSATs are a special case and take only a single attached craft, delivering one marine contingent.
  • If more units try to attach or deliver marines than these limits allow, the extra attempts automatically fail.
  • Grappling Claw ships are limited to one per structure section, and a section held by a Grappling Claw admits no Breaching Pods at all. Beyond that, only one vessel may grapple a Medium Ship or HCV, and a Capital Ship may be grappled by only two, which must attach to opposite ends. Bases and Enormous units have no overall limit - one claw per section is their only restriction.

While attached

  • A Breaching Pod stays attached to the structure block it hit until it chooses to Detach in the Movement Phase, or until the vessel is destroyed (as long as the pod's own structure location is not destroyed too). If the structure block a pod is attached to is destroyed before it detaches, the pod is automatically destroyed.
  • While attached, a pod matches its host's speed and heading and suffers -10 Initiative. When it detaches, it automatically faces away from its host.
  • Units can shoot at attached pods if they are in arc of the pod's structure location, rolling to hit as normal - but any shot aimed at a pod also automatically hits the vessel it is attached to. Weapons that can never damage a ship are the exception: hex-targeted weapons such as the Pak'ma'ra Plasma Web damage the pod only, and leave its host untouched.
  • A unit that ends its movement in the same hex as an Enormous unit and fails to attach to it - or does not try to attach at all - rams it instead, resolved at the end of the Firing Phase. Units that attach successfully never ram their host. Since bases do not move, attaching to one always succeeds, so only an attachment that is actively blocked (a full section, Advanced Armor, an Ancient hull) leads to a ram.

Delivering marines

After attaching, the pod tries to deliver its marines with another d10 roll on the table below, with a base 50% chance to board. Depending on the roll, marines who fail may be lost or return safely to their pod.

Delivery table (d10)
1-5Marines successfully delivered.
6-8Marines fended off, but return safely to their pod.
9+Marines fended off and eliminated.

Modifiers to the delivery roll:

  • +20% success: Yolu-specific bonus.
  • +10% success: Elite marines / Llort / target has Poor Crew / boarding the target's Primary section directly.
  • -10% success: Narn or Gaim defenders / target has Elite Crew or the Markab's Religious Fervor.

Missions

Marines who board a vessel start on their mission from the end of the following turn. Mission rolls can have modifiers too:

  • +10% success: Elite marines / target has Poor Crew.
  • -10% success: Narn or Gaim defenders / target has Elite Crew or the Markab's Religious Fervor.
  • Sabotage and Rescue only: a further -10% for every two turns the marines have been active aboard the enemy vessel.

Capture

The marines fight the defending marine contingents directly (the defenders are shown in the C&C's tooltip). This is simplified from the tabletop: each attacker makes just two rolls, one to see if it eliminates a defender (50% base chance) and one to see if it is eliminated (25% base chance). If the attackers defeat every defender, the enemy ship is disabled for the rest of the battle, as long as at least one attacking marine unit is still aboard. After a successful capture one marine unit stays on the ship, and the rest return to attached Breaching Pods if there are any.

Grappling claws

Some ships carry Grappling Claws, which attach and deliver marines largely under the same rules, with a few notable differences:

  • The attached unit cannot manoeuvre, but its host may, if the host is the same size as the attached ship or larger. The host's turn cost and turn delay become the sum of both units' values (a ship with a 1/3 turn cost attached to one with a 2/3 turn cost gives a total of around 1). The target unit moves the whole group when its turn comes in the initiative order, and may manoeuvre normally.
  • No more than one ship can grapple a Medium Ship or Heavy Combat Vessel. Two can grapple a Capital Ship, but they must be at opposite ends. One unit can attach to each section of an Enormous base.
  • If the structure a ship has grappled is destroyed during the battle (or the host itself is destroyed), the claw-equipped vessel breaks free and any claws still attached are destroyed. The destruction of either ship does not otherwise affect the other.
  • Once a ship has grappled its target, the target's hull blocks all fire through the claw's firing arcs (the attached ship cannot fire at its target, for safety reasons). Weapons that can fire into other arcs, or more widely, can still shoot at targets there. The host is not prevented from firing any weapon, except at the attached unit.
  • If a Grappling Claw is destroyed while it still holds marine units, they transfer to any surviving Grappling Claws on the ship. Marines in Grappling Claws count towards the marines defending the ship if an opponent tries to capture it.
  • The automatic extra hit on the host does not apply to shots at attached Grappling Claw ships.
  • Grappling Claw ships do not automatically face away from their host when they detach.
  • Called shots are possible in Fiery Void, as long as the weapon can make them. Ballistic weapons cannot, for example - with the exception of the Kor-Lyan Limpet Bore Torpedo.
  • To make a called shot, select the weapon you want to fire, then right-click the enemy ship to open its SCS and click the system you want to target. As long as every other condition is met (the system can be targeted by called shots, it is in the firing ship's arc and so on), it is now targeted, and your weapon's icon turns orange as usual to show it is locked in.
  • Called shots are usually made at -40% to hit, although some weapons get a bonus here, such as the Dilgar Point Pulsar.
Not the same as Reinforcements

A Delayed Deployment Slot is a scenario setting: the game is created knowing that a whole player slot turns up on a fixed turn, whatever happens in the battle, wherever its deployment zone allows. Reinforcements are bought by a player out of their own points, wait in hyperspace with no arrival turn at all, and only appear when one of their own jump-capable ships (or a jump gate) opens a jump point for them - on any turn, or never. Delayed Deployment is decided before the game starts; Reinforcements are played.

  • Set it up in Create Game, by setting a player slot's Deploys Turn field to the turn you want that slot to deploy - or "jump in". Ships cannot jump into hexes occupied by terrain or Enormous units, so make the deployment zone large enough!
  • You choose your entry hexes a turn early. A slot set to deploy on Turn 5 gets its Deployment Phase on Turn 4, where you position every arriving unit exactly as you would on Turn 1. Your units do not actually arrive until Turn 5 - until then they cannot be seen, targeted, moved or fired, and take no part in any other phase. A slot set to deploy on Turn 2 therefore picks its hexes during the normal Turn 1 Deployment Phase, alongside everyone else.
  • Your opponents get one turn of warning. From the moment you commit that Deployment Phase, every hex you chose is marked on the map for all players with a blue Jump Point hex. Arrivals no longer materialise out of nowhere: the enemy can see where a jump point is about to open, and has a turn to react. Only the hex is revealed, not what is coming through it (though the fleet list has always shown what a delayed slot contains, under a [Deploys on Turn N] header). A Jump Point appears even for stealthed units - the jump point itself is visible, even if what arrives through it may not be.
  • Any unit that has not arrived yet shows a cyan Deploying on Turn N banner in its ship window, and is listed in the fleet list under a [Deploys on Turn N] header.
  • Fighters and LCVs in a delayed slot can be deployed inside the hangars and rails of a carrier from the same slot, arranged in that slot's Deployment Phase in the usual way.
  • Ships that would normally set systems on Turn 1 (Hyach Specialists, for example) set them in their Deployment Phase - the turn they pick their entry hexes, not the turn they arrive. Systems set in Initial Orders (such as Vorlon Adaptive Armor) are set as normal on the turn the unit arrives.
  • Terrain, bases and OSATs cannot deploy later in the game: they always deploy on Turn 1, even in a slot set to deploy later. A slot containing any of these and delayed units gets a Turn 1 Deployment Phase for them and a second one later for the delayed units.

Every ship with a working Scanner generates Electronic Warfare (EW) points each turn, allocated in Initial Orders. Dedicated ELINT vessels - those carrying an ELINT Scanner - can also perform several longer-ranged sensor operations on top of the standard EW functions.

To allocate EW, select your ship and click an enemy or friendly unit to bring up the relevant EW buttons, then use Add / Remove (right-click sets a function to its maximum or to zero - see Hot Keys). EW lines are drawn on the map and can be toggled with the W / X / Y keys.

Advanced & Improved Sensors

Some factions field ships with upgraded sensors. Improved Sensors halve the effectiveness of enemy Jammers. Advanced Sensors ignore Jammers entirely, and also ignore enemy Blanket Protection, SDEW and Disruption, as well as defensive systems that lower a target's profile (shields, E-Web and so on).

  • Some units, such as terrain, are classed as Enormous. They block line of sight if any part of a shot would pass through their hex.
  • Ships that end their movement in the same hex as an Enormous unit automatically try to ram it (fighters are exempt).
  • Energy Mine damage, Mass Driver targeting and the power from an Improved Reactor also take Enormous size into account.
  • Fighters can escort a friendly ship in the same hex, using their guns to help intercept ballistic weapons aimed at it.
  • To escort a ship, the fighters must start and end their movement in its hex. When they do, they intercept ballistics on its behalf, as long as all the other conditions for interception hold: their weapons have an intercept rating, the incoming shot is in arc, the fighters are not jinking, and so on.

Carriers can launch and recover their fighters, shuttles and other small craft during a battle, and can start the battle with them docked inside rather than placed on the map. The options appear automatically on any ship with hangar space.

Default shuttles

  • Any hangar space not already taken by fighters, assault shuttles or breaching pods is filled automatically with Shuttles (or Minesweeping Shuttles, on ships with a minesweeper bonus).
  • Point at a Hangar system in the ship's SCS to see what it starts with - for example "Carrying: 2 / 14 slots" - and a list of the stored craft.

Deployment

In the Deployment Phase you can deploy fighters inside a ship's hangar: click the ship's hex and choose the blue Dock... option. If the fighters are already deployed in the carrier's hex, you can instead use the ship's Deploy Flights in Hangar tooltip button, or click the Hangar system icon in its SCS. Reinforcement flights arriving on later turns can dock into an already-deployed carrier the same way. Some fighters, such as Orieni Hunter-Killers, MUST start the game deployed in hangars.

Launching craft (Firing Phase)

  • Select one of your carriers and click the Launch button in its tooltip menu. A window lets you pick which stored craft to launch, and how many.
  • Shuttles are stored individually, but can be launched as a single flight of 1-6. Fighters launch as their stored flight (subject to the usual partial-flight rules).
  • The launch resolves at the end of the turn: the new flight appears in the carrier's hex with its heading and speed, facing the carrier's facing (plus an offset for side hangars).
  • You cannot launch from a carrier that is pivoting or rolling that turn.

Recovering and docking (Firing Phase)

  • Select one of your fighter flights and click Dock, or use the carrier's Recover button to pull craft in from the carrier's side.
  • A flight can only dock into a carrier in the same hex, on the same heading, at a compatible speed: the carrier must be at least as fast as the flight, and the speed difference within the flight's thrust rating.
  • The carrier needs a free hangar slot of a compatible type, must not be pivoting or rolling, and the hangar must not be destroyed.
  • If more than one eligible carrier is in the hex, you are asked to pick one. If a hangar cannot hold the whole flight, you are offered a split - dock some now and leave the rest in space.
  • Like launching, docking resolves at the end of the turn.

Capacity and penalties

  • Launch / land budget: each hangar has a per-turn capacity (its output) shared between launching and landing. A hangar with an output of 6 can launch 6 craft, recover 6, or any mix (launch 4 and recover 2, say) in one turn.
  • Initiative penalties: hangar operations are disruptive. A carrier that launches and/or recovers craft suffers -20 Initiative the following turn (just once, however many craft it moved). A freshly launched flight suffers -50 Initiative the turn after it launches. Both penalties clear by themselves.
  • Compatible hangar types: craft can only be stored in slots that fit them. Universal fighter bays take any size of combat fighter (and shuttles), but Assault Shuttles and Breaching Pods need their own dedicated slots, and custom-named fighters (such as Thunderbolts) are limited by each carrier's own capacity for that type.
  • Hangar damage: if a hangar takes damage, stored craft are destroyed along with it - empty slots and shuttles are lost first, then the cheaper craft.

When a carrier is destroyed

When a carrier is destroyed (other than by jumping to hyperspace), some of its docked fighters and shuttles may scramble out before the wreck goes up. The game rolls a d20:

Hangar escape (d20)
1-5No craft escape.
6-10A quarter of the docked craft escape (rounded down).
11-18Half escape (rounded down).
19-20All docked craft escape.
  • Only combat fighters, armed shuttle variants, Assault Shuttles and Breaching Pods can escape. The escapees are chosen by combat value (the most expensive craft first).
  • They appear in the carrier's final hex with its heading and speed, facing its final facing plus their hangar's launch direction, and suffer the usual -50 Initiative on their first turn (as if freshly launched).
  • Craft aboard a carrier that successfully jumped to hyperspace do NOT roll - they go with the jump and keep their full combat value.

Rearming docked craft

A flight that stays docked for a full turn automatically rearms its limited-ammunition weapons before relaunching:

  • Matter weapons (Slug Cannon, Gatling Gun and so on) regain 1 round per weapon per turn while docked, up to their starting load. This is free and automatic - no cost to the carrier.
  • Missiles need a Ballistic Ordnance Reserve bought for the carrier (see below). One missile per fighter per turn is restocked automatically, the most expensive type first, from the shared pool.
  • Marines (Breaching Pods) need Extra Marine Contingents bought for the carrier (see below). One marine unit per pod per turn is restocked automatically while docked, from the shared pool.
  • The turn a flight docks does not count: rearming starts on its first full turn inside the hangar.
  • When a flight is split on relaunch, the fighters with the most missiles (the ones that were restocked) are the ones launched first.

Ballistic Ordnance Reserve

Carriers in missile-using factions can buy an Ordnance Reserve enhancement in Fleet Selection, of up to 200 points.

  • The pool is shared across the whole carrier, and shown in the Hangar system's tooltip as "Ordnance Reserve: X / Y pts".
  • Each turn a missile or torpedo flight is docked (having been in for a full turn), the carrier spends points from the reserve equal to the PV of the missile type being restocked. The most expensive type is always refilled first.
  • The pool is one-way: spent points do not come back during the battle.
  • Only carriers with combat fighter hangar slots (not small hangars holding only shuttles) are offered the Ordnance Reserve.

Extra Marine Contingents

Any ship can buy Extra Marine Contingents in Fleet Selection: a pool of additional marine units used to restock docked Breaching Pods.

  • Each contingent costs 10 points and is a single marine unit.
  • Limit per ship: 1% of the ship's base Combat Point value, rounded up (a 600-PV ship can buy up to 6).
  • The pool is shared across the whole carrier, and shown in the Hangar system's tooltip as "Marine Contingents: X / Y".
  • Each turn a Breaching Pod flight has been docked for a full turn, it restocks one marine unit per pod from the pool, up to each pod's starting load (including any Extra Marine Units bought as an enhancement).
  • The pool is one-way: spent points do not come back during the battle.

Catapults

Some carriers have a Catapult instead of (or as well as) a standard hangar: a fixed, forward-firing launch rail that holds and launches a single superheavy fighter.

  • A catapult holds exactly one superheavy fighter; no other craft may launch from it or dock into it.
  • Its boxes are structural hit points only, not extra capacity - so, unlike a normal hangar's, extra boxes do not hold shuttles.
  • Launching: a catapult always launches its fighter straight ahead (at the carrier's current facing), with no initiative penalty - neither the usual -50 on the launched flight nor the -20 on the carrier. Launching works even if the catapult is damaged or destroyed.
  • Recovery: the fighter can only dock back into the catapult if it enters the carrier's hex from the rear - its heading must match the carrier's facing (it overtakes the carrier from behind). Like launching, recovery works however damaged the catapult is, but the carrier still suffers the usual -20 Initiative the following turn.
  • Landing on a damaged catapult: if any catapult boxes are destroyed when the fighter lands, it takes damage equal to the number of destroyed boxes. If it survives, it is stored with its damage and can be relaunched normally later. If the landing destroys it, it still counts as recovered and stored - but it can never be relaunched: the wreck blocks the catapult for the rest of the battle, and no replacement can be loaded.

Fighter Rails

Some carriers have external Fighter Rails instead of, or as well as, standard hangar bays.

  • Capacity: a rail's structure is also its capacity - a 6-structure rail carries up to 6 fighters. Rail boxes are part of the carrier's structure block they sit on, not a separate pool of hit points, so they add no hit points to the ship.
  • Compatible craft: combat fighters only (the type the ship's design declares, such as light fighters) - no shuttles, assault shuttles or breaching pods.
  • Launching: each fighter on a rail launches independently, like a normal hangar. The carrier suffers the usual -20 Initiative the following turn, but the launched flight gets no -50 Initiative penalty - it launches straight off the rail with no disorientation.
  • Recovery: fighters dock back onto a rail exactly as into a normal hangar - same hex, matching heading and speed - and the carrier still suffers -20 Initiative the following turn.
  • Rearming takes twice as long as in a standard hangar, because the airlocks between the rails and the carrier's interior are narrow: a docked flight starts rearming on its second full turn on the rail.
  • Structure damage (d20): whenever the structure block a rail is attached to takes damage in a turn, an unmodified d20 is rolled at the end of that turn. On a natural 16-20, one whole rail on that structure is destroyed (the smallest remaining rail is chosen automatically). Any fighters on it immediately try to escape, using the carrier-destruction escape table above - but escapees do suffer the -50 Initiative the following turn: a forced evacuation is not a clean launch.
  • Structure destroyed: if the structure block itself is destroyed, every rail attached to it is destroyed at the same time, and each rail's fighters roll to escape on the same d20 table.
  • Deployment: flights too large for any one rail are spread across several automatically (a 9-fighter flight on a carrier with 6-box and 3-box rails splits 6 + 3).

LCV Rails

Some carriers (such as the Deneth Deliverer) have LCV Rails: external docking collars that carry a whole Light Combat Vessel rather than a fighter flight. An LCV is a full ship in its own right, so an LCV Rail behaves differently from a fighter hangar.

  • Capacity: each LCV Rail holds exactly one LCV. Unlike Fighter Rails, an LCV Rail is an ordinary targetable system with its own hit points, its own entry on the carrier's hit chart, and it can be destroyed by direct fire.
  • Compatible craft: LCVs only. Fighters, shuttles and other small craft cannot dock to them, and an LCV cannot dock into an ordinary hangar or fighter rail.
  • Docking: the LCV must end its move in the same hex as the carrier, on a matching heading, with the carrier stationary, and with at least 1 thrust unspent. Dock with the LCV's own Enter Hangar button or the carrier's Recover button; you can choose which rail (Forward, Port, Starboard and so on) it docks onto.
  • Launching: a launched LCV is placed back in the carrier's hex with the carrier's facing and speed, and suffers -50 Initiative the following turn (it is briefly disoriented after release).
  • Penalties while docked: carrying LCVs makes a ship more sluggish. For each LCV docked, the carrier suffers -10 Initiative, and +1 thrust to the cost of a turn and to its turn delay. (The ship's printed turn cost does not change - only the thrust actually paid each turn: a carrier with four LCVs docked pays four extra thrust to turn.)
  • No rearming: unlike fighters in a hangar, a docked LCV does not rearm or reload - it simply sits on the rail until launched.
  • Landing on a damaged rail: if the rail has taken damage when the LCV docks, the LCV takes Structure damage equal to the rail's damage.
  • Rail destroyed while occupied: the LCV is forced to launch, and takes Structure damage equal to the rail's lost hit points plus 2d10 Matter damage from the debris.
  • Carrier destroyed: every docked LCV escapes the same way - forced to launch, taking the rail's damage plus 2d10.
  • Deployment: an LCV may not share a hex with another ship during deployment. To start the battle docked, select the LCV and click its carrier: the DOCK button puts it straight onto a chosen rail without placing it on the map. You can undock a deploy-docked LCV from the carrier's Deploy Flights in Hangar menu before committing; it then goes back to wherever it was placed, or waits to be placed.

Interception is defensive fire that makes an incoming shot harder to hit. Every weapon that can do it has an Intercept Rating.

When several weapons are put on the same shot, each one after the first is generally worth 5% less than its rating, cumulatively: three -20% weapons give -20%, -15% and -10%, for -45% in total rather than -60%. This degradation does not apply against ballistic weapons (missiles, captor mines) - every weapon assigned to a missile is worth its full rating.

Automatic interception

  • This is the default, and needs nothing from you. When the Firing phase resolves, the game gathers every intercept-capable weapon that has not fired that turn and assigns it to the incoming shots itself.
  • Firing a weapon offensively takes it out of the defensive pool for the turn: interception is always paid for with weapons you chose not to shoot with.
  • Weapons that take more than one turn to load are only used automatically if you first give your consent with a self-intercept marker - the green shield icon on the weapon in its ship window, in the Firing phase. Without it, the game will not spend a slow-charging weapon on defence.
  • Missile racks that reload in one turn and carry Interceptor missiles are switched to their interceptor mode and used automatically, drawing rounds from the magazine as they go. Racks with a longer loading time follow the marker rule above.
  • Automatic interception is the only way to intercept non-ballistic weapons, since you know nothing of those attacks until the Firing phase has resolved.
  • Mines do not intercept at all unless they carry the Command Controller enhancement.

Manual interception

Putting your own weapons on a specific incoming shot. This is declared in the Firing phase only:

  1. Click one of your own units to select it and open its tooltip. The INCOMING list at the bottom is every ballistic shot currently aimed at it, grouped under the ship that fired, written as "2x Heavy Missile (Class-H)".
  2. Select the weapon(s) you want to commit in the ship window, exactly as you would to fire them.
  3. The hit chance at the end of the row is the button. When your selection can legally commit to that row, the number is underlined in blue and the cursor becomes a pointer - click it to declare.
  • Hover over the hit chance at any time for the full to-hit breakdown, and - when it is refusing - the reason (No interceptor selected, Uninterceptable, No selected weapon can reach this shot and so on).
  • The hit chance drops as you commit, and the breakdown gains a Declared interception line. That number is your own declared orders only: automatic interception is worked out after the turn is committed and is deliberately not previewed, and your allies' uncommitted orders are not in your game data. It is not floored at zero either - a shot reading -25% is one you have already spent more on than it was worth.
  • Clicking a grouped row spends your selection greedily, best interceptor first: it stacks weapons on one shot of the group until that shot is fully suppressed, then moves on to the next. Use the ▶ caret to expand the group into one row per shot when you would rather place each weapon yourself.
  • A weapon selected to intercept manually cannot also fire offensively, and is not in the automatic pool either. So you cannot declare a manual intercept with a weapon and use its Self Intercept shield to put it in the automatic pool.
  • Weapons that can split their shots (Twin and Quad Arrays, Discharge Guns and so on) spend one gun per manual intercept; the rest are still free to fire, to intercept something else, or to be left to the automation. A weapon that cannot split commits all of its guns to the one shot you point it at.
  • Missile racks and other weapons that declare in an earlier phase can be hand-assigned in the Firing phase too, as long as they have fired nothing at all that turn. Ammunition is checked against the magazine before the order is offered.
  • To withdraw, clear that weapon's fire orders from the ship window in the usual way.

Meteor Defence

Using your defensive weapons against a Meteor Swarm (how swarms strike is under Terrain). This is declared in Initial Orders only, and is only offered while a Meteor Swarm is on the map:

  • Open an intercept-capable weapon's menu in the ship window and click the shield with the comet (☄). Right-click declares every similar weapon on the unit, or on every craft of a flight. A missile rack qualifies if it carries Interceptor missiles. The red shield withdraws the declaration, until you commit your orders.
  • A declared weapon is committed for the whole turn, whether or not a meteor ever arrives: it cannot fire, and cannot intercept in the Firing phase, manually or automatically. Its icon turns amber with a ☄. A weapon that takes more than one turn to load loses its charge, just as if it had fired.
  • When the unit enters a Meteor Swarm, each meteor that strikes has its damage reduced by the Intercept Rating of the declared weapons used against it - a rating of -10% blocks 2 points. The meteor still hits; only its damage is reduced.
  • Only weapons whose arc covers the unit's direction of motion into the swarm can defend. Each gun takes one meteor a turn. The best weapons are used first, and only until that meteor is cancelled, so the swarm's later meteors - and a second swarm - can still be defended against.
  • An Interceptor missile is only spent when it is actually used against a meteor.
  • Your opponent cannot see which weapons you declared until the swarms have been resolved. The combat log then records how many weapons defended, and how much damage they blocked.

What can be intercepted

  • Fire aimed at the intercepting unit itself, as long as the shot comes from within the weapon's firing arc. Arcs are measured from where the shot comes from - for a ballistic, that is the hex it was launched from, not wherever the shooter has moved to since.
  • Fire aimed at a friendly unit - but only by weapons with the free-intercept trait, and only when the protected unit lies between them and the incoming shot. Covering someone else is normally left to the automation, since the INCOMING list is shown on your own units' tooltips.
  • Fighter flights may protect the ship they are escorting from ballistic fire, if they end their movement in its hex and were in the same hex as the ship at the start of the turn. A flight cannot cover another flight.
  • Some attacks cannot be intercepted at all: laser weapons tend to be uninterceptable, for example, and proximity mines, rams and Molecular Slicers are others. Those rows refuse, with a reason, rather than disappearing from the list.

The Molecular Slicer prices interception out of its damage pool rather than per gun, and has its own way of declaring it - see the Shadow Association entry in Factions & Tiers.

A ship with a Jump Drive can open a jump point - a vortex into hyperspace that appears in a nearby hex for up to four turns - so that units can leave the battle by flying into it. Any unit may use any open jump point, including an enemy's, and including units with no Jump Drive of their own.

Two kinds of jump point

The colour tells you which. They are named from hyperspace's point of view, not the battle's:

Jump Point Entrance
Yellow: a doorway into hyperspace. Units fly into it to leave the battle. It is the one a ship opens with its own Jump Drive, and everything in this entry is about it.
Jump Point Exit
Blue: a doorway out of hyperspace. Reinforcements arrive through it. Nothing can go the other way - there is no Jump to Hyperspace button on a blue vortex, whoever opened it.

Opening a jump point

  1. In Initial Orders, select the Jump Engine in the ship window. It behaves like any other hex-targeted weapon: hovering it shows its reach as a yellow overlay on the map, and the overlay stays up while it is selected.
  2. Right-click a target hex within 4 hexes and choose Target selected weapons on hexagon. You need line of sight to the hex, and it must be clear of terrain, other jump points and Enormous units. Ships in the hex, friend or foe, do not block it.
  3. A control appears on the hex with a facing arrow and two turn buttons. The facing is the doorway - the hex side a unit has to come through to use the jump point. Step it round, then press OK.
  • Clicking away, or deselecting the Jump Engine, abandons the whole thing and no order is made. The facing cannot be changed afterwards; to re-aim, remove the fire order and declare again.
  • The declaration is private until all players commit their Initial Orders. It then shows to everyone as a yellow Jump Point Forming hex with the facing arrow over it, and the ship reads Jumping to Hyperspace in its own tooltip and ship window.
  • A jump point cannot be entered on the turn it is declared: it forms at the end of that turn, and is open from the next.
  • Opening a jump point reveals a stealthed or cloaked ship, just as using non-DEW EW does - a Shading Field or Cloaking Device drops for that turn.

Using a jump point

  • In the Movement phase, plot the unit's path so that it enters the jump point's hex through the side the arrow points at, then press Jump to Hyperspace on the unit's tooltip. Its movement ends there (any remaining thrust is lost), and it is removed at the end of the phase.
  • It is judged on the actual step that carried it into the hex, so a sideslip through the correct side works - and a unit that has been sitting in the hex since an earlier turn is judged on the step that first put it there.
  • Fighter flights may use a jump point too. Attached pods and docked craft go out with their host.
  • A unit that leaves through a jump point keeps its full combat value - it escaped, it was not destroyed - and its fleet-list row reads Jumped rather than Destroyed. Craft docked aboard a carrier that jumps go with it, and do not make the carrier-destruction escape roll.

Keeping it open

  • Maintaining: a jump point closes at the end of every turn unless its holder declares Maintain. Use the blue Jump Point ON/OFF switch in the Jump Engine's own system menu, in Initial Orders. Switching it ON makes the declaration and shuts the ship down for the turn - everything with a power cost except the Scanner and the Jump Engine itself - which is the price of holding the jump point open. Those systems cannot be switched back on until you switch Maintain OFF again.
  • A jump point also closes at the end of the turn if its holder ends the turn more than 4 hexes away, is destroyed, or leaves the battle (through this jump point or any other). It closes regardless after four turns open. Whatever the reason, it stays usable for the whole of the turn it closes on, and the reason is printed in that turn's Combat Log. The Initial Orders commit window warns you before you lose one you could still have kept.

Recharging

Opening a jump point spends the drive's whole charge. While a jump point stands, the Jump Engine's icon shows N/4 - the turns it has been open, out of four; the rest of the time it shows the ordinary charge counter. The drive starts a scenario fully charged, drops to zero when it opens a jump point, and recharges at 1 per turn from the turn after that jump point closes. It cannot open another until it is full again, which takes different times on different hulls (a Centauri Primus, for example, needs 16 turns).

Damaged Jump Drives

  • Damaged Jump Drives are dangerous. At the end of every turn a ship opens or maintains a jump point, it rolls d100 against the percentage of its Jump Engine boxes lost. Roll at or under it and the ship is destroyed outright - with any docked craft, which get no escape roll. The Combat Log records it. The risk is taken by the ship opening the jump point; units flying through one never roll.
  • The Jump Drive usually cannot be turned off unless it is seriously damaged, though some scenarios allow it. The game warns you if you try to deactivate it improperly (for example without Desperate rules, or before it is 50% damaged).

When the host ticks Reinforcements Allowed in Create Game, players may buy part of their fleet as reinforcements: units that start the battle in hyperspace and come out of a Jump Point Exit that one of their own ships opens during the game.

They come out of the same points as the rest of the fleet - a reinforcement is not free, it is a ship you have chosen not to bring to the party yet. They are not the same as a Delayed Deployment Slot, which arrives on a fixed turn set before the game began.

Buying them

  • In Fleet Selection the fleet has two groups, MAIN FLEET and REINFORCEMENTS. Pick the group before you buy, or use the Reinforce link on a bought row to move a unit between them. Saved fleets remember which group each unit was in.
  • Terrain, bases and OSATs cannot be reinforcements - they always deploy on Turn 1 - and the lobby will not let you put them in the group.
  • Bring a way in. At least one reinforcement needs a Jump Drive of its own, or your side needs a Jump Gate on the map. Without either, your reinforcements sit in hyperspace for the whole battle and their points are wasted - the lobby warns you before you click Ready.
  • What the enemy sees: nothing but a line in your fleet list reading Reinforcements - N units, X pts. Not which hulls, not what they carry, not who can open a jump point. You see your own in full, and can manage their power during Initial Orders by opening their ship windows from the FLEET INFO tab.

Calling them in

  1. In Initial Orders, press Manage Reinforcements. Every jump-capable unit you have in hyperspace is listed; choose one and press Choose Hex.
  2. Click the hex you want the jump point to open in, and set its facing with the arrow control.
  3. Tick the units that will ride through it - the Jump Point Manifest. The opening ship always rides its own jump point, and a unit already riding somebody else's is greyed out.
  • You can withdraw a declaration from the same window and start again. To change who rides without giving up the jump point itself, select the row and press Jump Manifest - it reopens the same tick list. Right-click any row, or its ⓘ, to open that unit's ship window.
  • The manifest also sets each unit's Speed as it leaves hyperspace (0-10, the opening ship's included), and the speed you pick counts for that turn's initiative.
  • For a fighter flight - or a ship a Docking Bay can take - pick a carrier from its Hangar list to have it arrive aboard that ship: the opening ship, or another unit riding the same jump point (which of the carrier's hangars it goes into is worked out for you). The room is shared, so a carrier that is full shows (no room), and unticking a carrier takes back whatever was riding in it (the manifest says so, and puts it on the map instead).
  • The declaration is public once orders are committed: everyone sees a blue hex with a facing arrow at the hex you named, for the rest of that turn. That warning is the price of arriving somewhere useful - the same deal a Delayed Deployment Slot gets.

Where it forms: the deviation roll

You will probably miss. At the end of the turn the jump point forms, and where it forms is a deviation roll against the opening ship's sensor rating: a d20, modified by:

  • -1 for the Minbari Federation;
  • -5 for an Ancient race (Vorlon, Shadow);
  • -3 for a friendly base or OSAT on the map;
  • -1 for a friendly ELINT vessel.

A low roll arrives exactly where you aimed; a high one scatters 1d3, 1d6, 1d10 or 2d10+2 hexes in a random direction, and the worst two bands turn the facing as well. An Ancient fleet with a base on the map arrives precisely about 40% of the time; a young race with a sensor rating of 10 is precise only on a natural 1. The Combat Log names the band, the roll and the distance, so you can see what happened. The jump point never forms inside terrain or an Enormous unit - if the dice put it there, it is nudged to the nearest legal hex.

Arriving

  • At the start of the next turn the wave is simply there - there is no Deployment Phase for it. Every unit stands in the jump point's hex on the jump point's facing, stacked, at the speed you chose in the manifest, and anything you ticked for a hangar is already aboard its carrier. If the jump point is no longer there, the units go back to hyperspace with nothing spent, and can be called in again later.
  • A Hyach ship arriving this way still gets a short Deployment Phase on its arrival turn - it is already in place - to choose its Specialists, which it can then use from that turn's Initial Orders.
  • Arriving is disorderly. A wave that comes out of hyperspace off course spends the turn sorting itself out: on its arrival turn only, every unit that rode that jump point takes an initiative penalty of 1 per hex it scattered, plus 2 for every 60° the facing was turned. A precise arrival costs nothing at all - one more reason the modifiers above are worth having. Only the first wave is disordered: a later wave through a jump point that is being held open comes out of a doorway that has already formed, and takes no penalty.

Holding the exit open

  • A ship's jump point closes at the end of the arrival turn unless the ship maintains it. Once on the board it can hold its jump point open exactly as it would a way out - Maintain Vortex on the Jump Engine, which shuts down all its powered systems except the Scanner, for at most four turns open. (A Vorlon pays its drive's power from the Power Capacitor instead, with no turn limit, and also pays it on the turn the jump point forms.)
  • On every turn it is maintained, Manage Reinforcements lists it with Select Reinforcements, so another wave can come through next turn; turning Maintain off cancels that wave.
  • A damaged drive rolls for failure on every turn it maintains the jump point, though never on the turn it forms. Once the jump point closes, the drive recharges and can then be used normally (including to open a way out).

Jump Gates

A fixed Jump Gate can be signalled to open a jump point inward instead of outward: click the gate in Initial Orders and choose Signal Gate for Arrival, which opens the same Manifest window.

  • A gate's jump point does not deviate - it opens in the gate's own mouth, on the gate's own facing, with no initiative penalty - and it stays open for its programmed hold, so it can bring a fresh wave through on every turn it stands. Pick the gate up again from Manage Reinforcements on later turns.
  • A gate belongs to nobody. If two players signal the same gate in the same turn, the one whose nearest unit is closest wins it, and the loser's manifest goes back to hyperspace with nothing spent. While the contest is live only the nearest side's marker is shown on the gate, so you can see whether you are going to get it.

Shadows and other phasing hulls

A Shadow ship does not tear a vortex open - it fades out, and now fades back in the same way. It declares a hex exactly as anything else does, and its arrival marker reads REINFORCEMENTS, but no jump point terrain ever appears: the ships simply are there on the arrival turn. Everything else - the deviation roll, the manifest, the arrival speed, the initiative penalty - is identical, except that only fighters its hangars can take (and ships its Docking Bay can) may ride with it, and they always arrive aboard. Such a drive still cannot open a way out; it uses the old one-click Jump to Hyperspace for that.

One-way

You cannot go back out the way you came. A blue Jump Point Exit is one-way: to leave the battle you need a yellow Jump Point Entrance, which means opening one with a drive that has recharged.

There are three types of mine in Fiery Void - Captor, Direct Energy Weapon (DEW) and Proximity - described below.

Buying and deploying mines

  • In a game with Mines Allowed ticked in Create Game, you can buy mines in Fleet Selection from your faction's list.
  • Unlike ships, mines can be bought in batches: Add to fleet lets you choose how many of that type to buy, along with any enhancements (see Mine Enhancements in Ammo, Options & Enhancements).
  • Taking any mines at all costs a 100-point premium, plus +10% to the unit cost of all mines for each type of mine taken after the first (of the three: Captor, DEW and Proximity).
  • Once the game starts you can place mines one by one, or use Deploy Minefield to scatter any number at random within an area you choose. Mines can go anywhere on the map, as long as it is not within 10 hexes of an enemy deployment zone.

Mine ranges

Once deployed, mines are stealthed: they cannot be seen by enemies until they attack or are detected. On the turn a mine is deployed or spawned you can tailor its ranges - against Capitals/HCVs, MCVs/LCVs and Fighters - from 0 up to its maximum, by clicking the mine's weapon icon in the Deployment or Pre-Turn Orders phase. Once set, the ranges apply for the rest of the game; if you do not set them, they stay at their maximum. The propagate buttons in the Mine Settings menu set the ranges of every mine of the same type at once.

Detecting mines

To detect mines, assign EW to Detect Mines in Initial Orders - an option that only appears if your opponent has mines in the game. Fighters and shuttles can do this too, converting their Offensive Bonus (OB) into Detect Mines points at 10 OB per point (OB used this way is not available for firing later in the turn). Detection depends on:

  • the Detect Mines EW assigned;
  • any Minesweeper Bonus;
  • the distance to the hidden enemy mine;
  • the mine's signature.

Detection happens at the END of each committed Movement segment - not during a unit's movement, so that players cannot detect a mine and then take their moves back. A mine is revealed if your Detect Mines EW plus Minesweeper Bonus is GREATER THAN the distance to the mine plus the mine's signature.

Detecting a mine does not tell you what type it is: to find out, scan it with at least 1 OEW after it has been detected.

Shooting at mines

On top of the usual modifiers, a few apply only to mines:

  • All mines have a basic profile of 60, minus 5 per point of signature - so a mine with Signature 3 has a basic profile of 45.
  • The firing ship's Detect Mines EW for the turn is added to any Minesweeper Bonus it has, and acts as a "general lock-on".
  • If that general lock-on is greater than the mine's distance plus signature, the difference (times 5) is added to your hit chance. For example: with 10 EW on mine detection, a Signature 1 mine at range 3 needs 4 to detect, so the detecting ship has +6 (times 5) to hit it - +30%. It still suffers the double range penalty if it has no OEW lock-on on the mine, as usual.
  • Unless a mine has the Command Controller enhancement, it does not use its weapons to intercept.

How mines attack

All mines automatically attack the first unit that comes into range - allies included, unless the mine or mine launcher has the Identify Friend or Foe enhancement.

Captor mines
Launch at the first viable target that comes within range during movement, then resolve the attack as a ballistic weapon in the Firing Phase - so they can be intercepted like other ballistics. Units with Jammers halve the range at which a captor mine attacks them. A captor mine is destroyed whether it hits or not. The Kor-Lyan and the WotCR Abbai have ships with Ballistic Mine Launchers, which can leave lingering captor mines if they do not find a target straight away.
Proximity mines
Explode and damage the first viable target that comes within range during movement, resolving the attack just before the Firing Phase, like terrain collisions. They always hit their target, and are destroyed after attacking.
DEW mines
Carry weapons, which they fire automatically at the first viable target they meet in the Movement phase; the attacks are resolved normally in the Firing Phase. A DEW mine is detected once it fires, and must normally fire all its weapons at the same target. With no EW of its own it gets no weapon lock, but it does get an accuracy bonus to its shots. Once detected, its signature drops to a lower value.

Skin dancing is flying just metres above the surface of a large unit or base - a very dangerous manoeuvre, performed only by the most agile ships.

Who can skin dance

Any unit able to skin dance automatically tries to when it ends its movement in the same hex as an Enormous unit (but not a terrain unit, where the normal collision rules in Terrain apply instead). To be eligible, a unit:

  • must be a Medium Ship or smaller, and agile (fighters and shuttles count as agile for this). There are rare exceptions, such as Torvalus capital ships;
  • cannot be stationary, while the Enormous unit cannot be moving faster than Speed 5 - and if the Enormous unit is moving, the dancer must be moving in the same direction or the exact opposite one;
  • must not be a unit designed to ram, such as the Orieni Hunter-Killer drones.

The roll

Eligible ships roll a d20 just before the Firing Phase, and must roll 15 or less to succeed. Modifiers:

  • +1 for each 2 points of speed (or part of 2) the dancer is going above Speed 5.
  • +5 if either unit is rolling or pivoting. These add up: a dancer rolling while its target pivots is +10. A rotating base does not count as pivoting.
  • +1 for each point of thrust rating the dancer has lost with destroyed thrusters (wherever they are) - a ship that has lost two of its 4-rating aft thrusters is +8.
  • +3 for each level a skin-dancing fighter is jinking.
  • -1 for a fighter flight with a navigator.

Results

  • If the roll fails by 5 or less (a modified 16-20), the dance is aborted with no ill effects and no chance of a ram. On a 21 or higher, the dancer smashes into the Enormous unit's hull. For fighters, one fighter at random crashes into the hull while the others break away; the survivors cannot fire (even defensively) or guide weapons that turn, being too busy pulling out of the manoeuvre.
  • A successful dancer cannot be fired on by enemy units unless they skin dance over the same target too - except by ballistic weapons launched at it earlier in the turn. The vessel being skimmed cannot fire at the dancer either, nor fire defensively against its weapons, because the dancer is inside its weapons' tracking zones.
  • The dancer's forward-firing weapons (those that can legally fire into the row of hexes directly ahead) all automatically roll the best result on their to-hit dice - a 1 on the d100 - so they hit in almost every case. In fact those weapons may not fire at any other target (except other skin dancers, against which they fire normally) unless they can fire outside the 120° forward area. If they can, they may choose another eligible target instead. The skimmed unit may use intercept fire and other defensive devices normally.
  • Fighters cannot guide missiles or other ballistic weapons at a target while skin dancing (even the unit they are dancing over), even with a navigator: the pilot and crew are too busy flying the fighter and its onboard weapons.

Asteroids
Single hex only. Added in Create Game, or by hand from the Terrain faction's list. They block line of sight, and cause 1d10 × Speed raking damage to any unit that moves through them or ends its movement in their hex. The damage is dealt just before the Firing Phase.
Moons / large asteroids
Anything larger than an asteroid - occupying several hexes. Units moving into their area automatically crash into them, during the Pre-Firing Phase.
Dust fields and dust clouds
Added in Create Game with the Dust count, and placed at random: most are a single hex, but about one in six is a Dust Cloud of 3, 5 or 7 hexes. Dust does not block line of sight. Every dust hex a unit enters damages it, during the Pre-Firing Phase:
  • Ships take speed / 2 (fractions dropped) to the Structure of the side that entered the hex - the Primary Structure if that side has none.

A weapon's damage type decides what its damage actually does once it lands.

Point at a weapon in the ship window and its mode is shown as Damage type. Some weapons have more than one (the Minbari Neutron Laser can fire as Raking or Piercing, for example): click the firing mode button in the weapon's menu to switch, or right-click it to switch every similar weapon that has no orders yet. Linked and Sustained are a little different from the rest - they are extra rules layered on top of a weapon's damage type, rather than damage types of their own.

Standard

The simplest damage type, and the one the others are measured against.

  • The whole shot lands on one system, rolled on the hit chart of the section facing the shooter. Against a fighter flight, it hits one fighter.
  • The system's armour is subtracted once, and the rest is dealt as damage.
  • Any damage left over once the system is destroyed spills over (overkills) into that section's Structure, and from there into the Primary Structure.

Flash

An explosion rather than a beam, so it hurts everything nearby - a Plasma Wave torpedo or a Mag Gun, for example.

  • The target is hit exactly as by a Standard shot.
  • On top of that, every other unit in the same hex - friend or foe - takes 25% of the shot's damage (each unit's own armour still applies). Mines are unaffected.
  • Against a fighter flight, the fighter hit takes the full damage, and every other fighter in the flight takes a quarter.
  • Overkill does not go to Structure: it is rolled again on the hit chart instead. Flash damage cannot reach Primary systems until every system on an outer structure block has been destroyed.
  • Think twice before firing one at an enemy sharing a hex with your own fighters!

Linked

Mostly found on fighter guns mounted in pairs or threes, such as the Plasma Blaster.

  • All the shots share one to-hit roll, so they either all hit or all miss.
  • Every shot that hits strikes the same system (or, against a flight, the same fighter).
  • Each shot's damage is still worked out separately, so the target's armour is subtracted from every shot.

Piercing

A shot that punches straight through the target and out the far side.

  • Against a ship with more than one Structure block, the damage is split into three parts: one for the section facing the shooter, one for the Primary section, and one for the section on the opposite side.
  • If the shot goes in and out through the same section (a Heavy Combat Vessel hit from the side, for example), it is split into two parts instead: that section and Primary.
  • Damage left over after destroying a system is lost - Piercing shots never overkill.
  • Against smaller units with only one Structure block (Medium Ships and below) there is nothing to pass through: the shot lands as a single hit, and its damage is reduced by 10%.
  • Piercing damage usually cannot be aimed at fighters at all.

Pulse

A rapid burst of identical bolts, such as the Medium Pulse Cannon.

  • The whole burst makes one to-hit roll. If it misses, every pulse misses.
  • If it hits, the weapon scores a random number of pulses, plus a bonus pulse for every step the roll beat the number it needed, up to a maximum. The weapon's tooltip spells it out: "Pulse damage: D5, +1/20%, max. 6 pulses" means 1-5 hits, plus one more for every full 20% the roll was under the target number - but never more than 6.
  • Each pulse is a separate hit that does its full damage and picks its own system, so a good roll can spread damage across a ship - or across several fighters in a flight.
  • Interception lowers the chance to hit, and so also cuts down the bonus pulses.

Raking

A beam swept across the target, typical of lasers.

  • The damage is broken into chunks called rakes, normally 10 points each (the weapon's tooltip says so if its rakes are a different size). A 27-point hit, for example, becomes rakes of 10, 10 and 7.
  • Each rake rolls for its own system in the same section, so one shot can damage several systems.
  • Armour only has to be cut through once: if a later rake strikes a system an earlier rake has already hit, the armour already cut through does not count again.
  • Against fighters, Raking works just like Standard - all the damage goes on one fighter.

Sustained

A beam held on the target over several turns.

  • Some weapons, such as the Heavy Laser, can be overcharged in Initial Orders: after a full recharge cycle spent overcharging, their next shot is Sustained. A few, such as the Particle Cutter, are always in Sustained mode.
  • A Sustained weapon fires on consecutive turns, usually two (some manage three). It must keep firing in its default mode each turn (usually raking), or the rest of the sequence is lost.
  • If a shot hits, next turn's shot at the same target hits automatically and cannot be intercepted. If it misses, or you switch targets, the next shot rolls to hit as normal.
  • Follow-up shots ignore any armour earlier shots have already cut through on the systems they hit, and the target's shields do not reduce them a second time.
  • After its final shot the weapon is forced offline for one turn to cool down, then starts recharging from scratch.
